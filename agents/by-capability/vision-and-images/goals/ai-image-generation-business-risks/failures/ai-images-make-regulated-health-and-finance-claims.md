**Frequency**: Unknown

## Issue: A business uses an image or video tool to make a before-and-after shot, a "results" photo, an AI doctor or a lifestyle scene, and the picture states a health, body or money result that no record supports. Treat every result shown in an image as a claim: no generated result passed off as real, proof on file before the ad runs, typical results only, and a qualified reviewer for health and earnings claims.

> **Read this first:** This page is design guidance for an ad review workflow. It is not legal or medical advice. Statements about the FTC, state attorneys general and the UK Advertising Standards Authority summarize the sources in section 4 as read on October 29, 2026. Some are law-firm summaries or press releases, not rulings. Several cases did not involve AI. Whether a given image makes a claim, and what proof is enough, is for a qualified lawyer and, for health claims, a qualified clinical reviewer.

> **One line:** A weight-loss clinic asks its ad tool for "a before-and-after of a woman who lost 40 pounds on our program" and runs the result, so the ad shows a transformation no patient had, with a made-up nurse beside it; the fix is a claims gate that tags any image showing a health, body or money result, blocks generated results presented as real people's results, requires proof of typical results on file before launch, and sends the ad to a qualified reviewer.

## 1. In plain words
An image can say "this works" without a single word. A before-and-after pair says the product changed a body. A smiling "nurse" says a professional backs it. A beach house says the business opportunity pays. Image tools make all three in seconds, and none of them is a real customer, a real nurse or a real payout. This page is narrower than [AI product images that show features it does not have](./ai-product-images-show-features-it-does-not-have.md), which covers what the product looks like and hands result claims to a reviewer in its rule 5. This page is that review: results, people who vouch for them, and the proof behind them.

## 2. What it looks like
The clinic, people and IDs are invented. The rules quoted in the "after" are the ones section 5 sets out.

**Without the fix**
```
Marketer:  Make a before-and-after for our GLP-1 program. Woman, 40s, Tampa. Lost 40 lbs in 8 weeks.
Ad tool:   Here are 4 options. Option 2 has the strongest contrast. Add a nurse in scrubs giving a thumbs-up?
Marketer:  Yes. Caption: "Real results from Sunline patients."
Ad tool:   Done. Scheduled on Facebook and Instagram, $3,000 budget, starts today.
           (Neither the woman, the nurse nor the 40 pounds exists. No proof file. No review.)
```
**With the fix**
```
Marketer:  Make a before-and-after for our GLP-1 program. Woman, 40s, Tampa. Lost 40 lbs in 8 weeks.
Ad tool:   I can't make this one. A before-and-after shows a result, and a generated person's result
           would read as a real patient's. "40 lbs in 8 weeks" needs typical-results proof on file first.
           1) Use consented, dated photos of a real patient plus your typical-results data
           2) Make a product or clinic image with no body result shown
           3) Send to claims review (Dr. Patel)   4) Something else
Marketer:  2, and send the result ad to review.
Ad tool:   Made a clinic-lobby image, tagged "no result shown." Result ad held as CR-418 for Dr. Patel.
```
In the fixed version the tool names the claim the picture makes, refuses to generate a result as if real, offers paths that keep the campaign moving, and routes the claim to a named reviewer.

## 3. Why it happens
- **Image tools take the result as a prompt, not a claim.** "Lost 40 lbs" is a style instruction to the generator. Nothing in the tool knows the number is a promise that needs proof, and copy review often checks words only. The FTC's health guidance says the before-and-after images in its Example 6 "likely convey a claim" even without the product name.
- **Generated people look like witnesses.** A tool that can make a patient can make a nurse, a pharmacist or a happy customer. The Connecticut release describes AI figures posing as nurses, pharmacists and law enforcement to back weight-loss claims. The viewer reads them as real people vouching for a real result.
- **Money claims hide in scenery.** A mansion, a sports car or a stack of cash is a common "success" prompt. The FTC said Herbalife's materials showed distributors "enjoying expensive houses, luxury cars, and exotic vacations" next to income promises. A generated lifestyle scene makes the same implied claim, and nobody tags it as one.

## 5. The fix
**The principle:** A result shown in a picture is a claim. It is a real, documented, typical result with proof on file and a qualified reviewer's sign-off, or it is not shown.

**Do this: advertisers, agencies and ad tools**
1. **Tag result claims at brief and at output.** Every image brief and every output is tagged for: body or health change, before-and-after, named condition, earnings or income, investment return, credential (doctor, nurse, pharmacist, advisor), testimonial. A tool checks the output too, because the image can imply a result the brief did not name.
2. **No generated result presented as real.** An AI-generated or AI-altered person is never shown as a customer, patient or earner with a result. Result images use real, consented customers with dated, unaltered photos on file.
3. **No generated credentials.** AI-generated people are not shown as doctors, nurses, pharmacists, financial advisors or officials, and not in scrubs, white coats or badges that imply one.
4. **Proof on file before launch.** Each tagged claim links to a proof file: for health claims, the evidence the reviewer accepts; for results, records of what users actually achieved. No link, no launch.
5. **Typical results only.** The result shown matches what the proof file says users generally get. A bigger result does not run with "Results not typical" in small print.
6. **Lifestyle scenery is an earnings claim.** Mansions, luxury cars, yachts, cash and exotic travel in ads for income, trading or business opportunities are tagged as earnings claims and need rule 4 proof, or are removed.
7. **Regulated categories go to a qualified reviewer.** Prescription and weight-loss drugs, medical devices, supplements with health claims, investment and income offers: a named clinical or legal reviewer signs off. The tool may not approve these.
8. **Fix the image, not the fine print.** A failed check is fixed by changing or dropping the image. A disclaimer or an "AI-generated" label does not make a fake result acceptable.
9. **Log every decision.** Asset ID, source (real, AI-edited, AI-generated), claim tags, proof file ID, reviewer, decision and date.

**Reference design: decision table**
| Image shows | Source | Proof on file? | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product, setting or mood, no result | Any | Not needed | Publish under the normal review |
| Body, health or skin result, or before-and-after | AI-generated or AI-altered person | Any | Do not publish |
| Body or health result | Real consented customer, unaltered | Typical-results proof | Clinical or legal reviewer decides |
| Doctor, nurse, pharmacist or advisor | AI-generated | Any | Do not publish |
| Lifestyle wealth scene in an income or investment ad | Any | Earnings proof | No proof: remove scene; else legal reviewer decides |
| Regulated category product | Any | Any | Reviewer sign-off required |

**Moves** (see [conversation moves](../../../../human-machine-interface/solutions-library/conversation-moves.md); most preferred first)
| Move | When, in this pattern |
|---|---|
| Lead with the answer | The tool says first which claim the image makes and why it cannot generate it |
| Offer choices: 2-4 options plus "Something else", free text open | A result image is blocked: real patient photos with proof, a no-result image, send to review |
| Ask one clarifying question | Tags are unclear: "Does this image show a result a customer got, or only the product?" |
| Hand off to a person | Any regulated category, any real-customer result, any earnings scene |

**Don't over-correct.** Product shots, clinic or office scenes, ingredient close-ups and lifestyle images that show no result stay allowed. A real patient's consented, unaltered, typical result with proof can run after review. A fitness or finance brand is not banned from AI images; only the result, the credential and the wealth scene need the gate. Do not tag every smiling person as a testimonial, and do not block plain color correction of a real photo.

**Hand-off rule.** Every tagged result, credential or earnings image in a regulated category goes to the named clinical or legal reviewer with the image, its source tag, the claim tags and the proof file. It stays held until the reviewer logs a decision; untagged assets in the same campaign proceed.

**Framework used:** none yet. Rules 4 and 5 follow the FTC health guidance and the NextMed order's typical-results requirement; rule 6 follows the FTC's Herbalife statement; rules 2 and 3 follow the practices the attorneys general described. The tags, table and gate are our judgment.

## 9. Common questions
### Are AI-generated before-and-after photos legal in ads?
No opened source answers this for AI images directly. The FTC treats before-and-after images as claims that need proof, and it acted against NextMed over before-and-after photos of non-customers. A generated person's "result" is not any customer's result. Ask counsel.

### Can I use an AI-generated doctor or nurse in a health ad?
This page says no. The attorneys general letter describes AI figures posing as nurses and pharmacists as a deceptive practice. A real, consented professional can appear after review.

### Does an "AI-generated" label or "results not typical" make it OK?
Not on this page. The FTC health guidance says "Results not typical" does not cure deceptive testimonials, and the ASA says advertisers stay responsible however the ad was made.

### Can a picture of a mansion be an earnings claim?
The FTC's Herbalife statement describes income promises shown with pictures of expensive houses, luxury cars and exotic vacations. Treat such scenes in income or investment ads as earnings claims and get review.
