## Issue: AI image and video generators produce recognizable copyrighted characters when a user names them, and also when a user types only a few generic words that point to them.

> **Read this first:** Statements about copyright and lawsuits on this page summarize the sources cited here. Lawsuits are described only as filed allegations; no court ruling is reported or predicted. This is not legal advice and needs review by a qualified person, such as a copyright lawyer, before anyone relies on it.

> **One line:** A user types "videogame, plumber" or "Shrek at a birthday party" and the tool returns a studio's character ready to download; the fix is that the product checks both the request and the finished image against a list of known protected characters, refuses with a working alternative, and logs every match.

## 1. In plain words
A user asks an image or video tool for a famous cartoon character, or just for "a yellow sponge who lives under the sea", and gets back a picture of the real character. The tool never checked whether the picture copies a character someone owns. The user may put it on a T-shirt, an ad or a book cover without knowing the risk.

## 2. What it looks like
The sources do not publish exact prompts and replies from a named product, so the products below are generic.

**Without the fix (image generator, name given)**
```
User:  Shrek and Donkey at a kid's birthday party, 3D cartoon style, for my party invite.
Tool:  Here are 4 images. Want me to upscale one?
User:  Upscale number 2 and add "Jayden turns 6!"
Tool:  Done. Download ready (2048 x 2048).
```
**Without the fix (no name given)**
```
User:  videogame, plumber
Tool:  Here are 4 images.   [all four show a red-capped, mustached plumber in overalls]
```
**With the fix**
```
User:  videogame, plumber
Tool:  These images closely match a well-known video game character, so I didn't
       return them. I can do one of these instead:
       1) An original plumber hero with your own colors and outfit
       2) A generic retro pixel-art plumber that doesn't copy any character
       3) Something else
User:  2, blue overalls, green cap.
Tool:  Here are 4 images. (Character check: no match.)
```
In the fixed version the check runs on the finished image, not only the words, so "videogame, plumber" is caught even though no name was typed, and the user still gets something usable.

## 3. Why it happens
- **Name filters miss descriptions.** He et al. found that a 60-word description with no name produced about 48% of their 50 characters, and that two generic words such as "videogame, plumber" were enough for Mario (section 4). A filter that only blocks names never sees these.
- **Prompt rewriting is not enough on its own.** In the same paper, rewriting the prompt alone cut detected characters on one model from about 30 to about 14 of 50, not to zero.
- **Nothing checks the output.** A prompt check runs before the image exists. If no step compares the finished image or video to known characters, a match goes straight to the user. The studios allege Midjourney could filter outputs as it does violent or sexual content and chose not to (Decrypt, section 4).
- **Video tools inherit the problem.** An image-to-video feature that animates an uploaded or generated picture of a character repeats the copy in motion. Warner Bros. alleges Midjourney once blocked video from many such images, then lifted that block (section 4).

## 5. The fix
**The principle:** A generator never hands over an image or video that matches a known protected character unless a license on file covers it, and it checks the output, not only the words.

**Do this: image and video generator products**
1. **Check the request.** Block or rewrite requests that name a protected character, franchise or studio, using a maintained list of names, nicknames, common misspellings and the keywords that most often appear with each name.
2. **Check the output.** Before delivery, compare every image, and sampled frames of every video, to reference images of the protected characters on the list. A match above the block level is withheld, even when no name was typed.
3. **Check what gets animated.** An image-to-video or edit request runs the same output check on the uploaded or source image before animating it.
4. **Refuse with a next step.** Every withheld result says why in one plain sentence ("closely matches a well-known character") and offers 2-4 options: an original character, a generic version, or "Something else". It never lectures or accuses the user.
5. **Unlock only with a license.** An account with a license record for a character (for example a studio's own team) can generate it; the output carries the record ID in its metadata.
6. **Send unsure matches to review.** A match between the review level and the block level is held for a person, or delivered with a plain notice, by a policy the business sets; it is never silently delivered.
7. **Log every match.** Log the prompt, the matched character, the score, the action taken and any license ID, so the business can answer a rights holder's complaint and tune the list.

**Reference design: decision table**
| Request names a protected character? | Output matches a protected character? | License on file? | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| No | No | n/a | Deliver |
| Yes or No | Yes, above block level | Yes, covers this use | Deliver; log license ID |
| Yes or No | Yes, above block level | No | Withhold; refuse with options; log |
| Yes | No (rewrite worked) | No | Deliver the rewritten result; tell the user it was changed; log |
| Yes or No | Unsure (between review and block levels) | No | Hold for review or deliver with notice, per policy; log |

**Moves** (see [conversation moves](../../../../human-machine-interface/solutions-library/conversation-moves.md); only the moves that apply, most preferred first)
| Move | When, in this pattern |
|---|---|
| Offer choices: 2-4 options plus "Something else", free text open | An output is withheld for matching a protected character |
| Admit uncertainty and show how to check | The match is unsure: the tool says so instead of silently delivering or blocking |
| Hand off to a person | A rights holder complains, or a user with a license disputes a block |

**Don't over-correct.** Do not block every cartoon, plumber, mouse, ogre or superhero. "A green ogre in a swamp" or "a caped hero over a city" should work when the output does not match a listed character. Do not block characters with a license on file, a user's own original character uploaded as a reference, or news and commentary images handled under normal policy. Do not treat a public-domain figure or a generic style word (such as "anime" or "pixel art") as a protected character. Track wrongly blocked requests as a counter-metric.

**Hand-off rule.** A rights holder's complaint, or a user's appeal of a block, goes to a person with the logged prompt, output, match score and any license record; the person, not the model, decides.

**Framework used:** none yet. Rules 1 and 2 follow the prompt-rewriting, negative-prompt and output-detection setup tested in He et al. (section 4), plus our judgment.

### Detection signals (proposed, not yet calibrated)
| Signal | Where it shows | Counter-signal (what it could be instead) |
|---|---|---|
| Prompt contains a listed character, franchise or studio name | Request log | A licensed account, or text about the character with no image of it asked for |

## 9. Common questions
### Can AI image generators make Disney characters?
Some can. Disney and Universal's lawsuit alleges Midjourney produced images of characters such as Yoda, Minions and Shrek. Those are allegations; no ruling is reported in the sources here.

### Why does an AI make Mario when I never typed "Mario"?
He et al. found two generic words, "videogame, plumber", were enough for one set of models. Models learn which words go with which characters, so a name filter alone misses it.

### Is it legal to sell AI art of a copyrighted character?
This page does not answer that. The sources here are filed lawsuits with no rulings. Ask a qualified copyright lawyer before selling such images.

### How do AI tools stop copyrighted characters?
He et al. found prompt rewriting plus negative prompts cut detected characters the most, but not to zero. This page adds an output check against known characters before delivery.

### Can a studio make images of its own characters with these tools?
Yes, if the tool keeps a license record for the account. Rule 5 lets licensed accounts generate the character and tags the output with the license ID.
