**This page is a dated teardown of one public deepfake fraud case, for finance teams and for builders of finance-ops assistants that draft or release payments.** The company was Arup, a British engineering firm. The user was a finance worker in its Hong Kong office. The moment was a group video call in January 2024 in which the "CFO" and other staff were faked, followed by 15 transfers. **Non-US case (Hong Kong, with a UK head office).**

**Status: Needs review.** Built from sources downloaded on 2026-10-29 and read as text: two CNN articles (February 4 and May 17, 2024), The Guardian (May 17, 2024), The Standard of Hong Kong (February 4, 2024), The Register (February 5, 2024), and a written reply by Hong Kong's Secretary for Security to the Legislative Council (June 26, 2024), which is the only government document we opened. We did not open a Hong Kong police press release, the Financial Times report that first named Arup, or RTHK. Statements about police findings and charges summarize those sources; they are not legal advice and need review by a qualified person.

The file name says February 2024 because that is when Hong Kong police first described the case in public. The transfers and the police report happened in January 2024; Arup was named in May 2024.

## What happened (as reported)

- **The opening message.** CNN (Feb. 4, 2024), citing senior superintendent Baron Chan Shun-ching via the broadcaster RTHK, says the worker got a message "purportedly from the company's UK-based chief financial officer." At first he "suspected it was a phishing email, as it talked of the need for a secret transaction." The Legislative Council reply says the email impersonated the CFO of "the informant's head office in the United Kingdom" and invited the worker to a group video conference "for some alleged confidential transactions."
- **The call.** CNN reports police said the worker attended a video call with "what he thought were several other members of staff, but all of whom were in fact deepfake recreations." Chan told RTHK: "(In the) multi-person video conference, it turns out that everyone [he saw] was fake." The worker "put aside his early doubts after the video call because other people in attendance had looked and sounded just like colleagues he recognized."
- **How the fake was made, per police.** The Legislative Council reply says police believed the call "was generated using downloaded online public video clips and voices of the impersonated officer," that it was pre-recorded, so "there was actually no interaction between the informant and the fraudster," and that the fraudster then "ended the meeting under some pretext" and "continued with the payment transfer instructions using instant messaging software." The Register quotes Chan: "I believe the fraudster downloaded videos in advance and then used artificial intelligence to add fake voices to use in the video conference."
- **Other channels.** The Standard (Feb. 4, 2024) says officers reported the scammer "even utilized WhatsApp, email, and 'one-to-one' video conferences to communicate with Hong Kong staff members," and that "other employees of the same company were also instructed to attend the meeting."
- **The money.** CNN: a total of "$200 million Hong Kong dollars - about $25.6 million." The Standard: "15 transactions totaling HK$200 million to five local accounts within a week." The Guardian gives the amount as HK$200m (£20m). The Legislative Council reply: "a loss of about HK$200 million" to "five local bank accounts."
- **Discovery.** CNN (Feb. 4): the scam "was only discovered when the employee later checked with the corporation's head office."
- **The company is named.** CNN (May 17, 2024) says an Arup spokesperson told CNN that Arup "notified Hong Kong police in January" and confirmed "fake voices and images were used." The Guardian says the Financial Times first reported that Arup was the company. Arup's statement, as quoted by both: "Our financial stability and business operations were not affected and none of our internal systems were compromised."
- **Police status.** The Guardian (May 17) quotes a Hong Kong police statement that the employee was "deceived of some HK$200m," that no arrests had been made, and that the case was classified as "obtaining property by deception." The Legislative Council reply (June 26) says the case "is still under Police investigation."

## What each party said

- **Arup.** Spokesperson, to CNN: "we can't go into details at this stage as the incident is still the subject of an ongoing investigation." Rob Greig, global chief information officer, in the same emailed statement: operations are "subject to regular attacks, including invoice fraud, phishing scams, WhatsApp voice spoofing, and deepfakes," and "the number and sophistication of these attacks has been rising sharply in recent months." The Guardian reports Greig hoped the case would "raise awareness." An internal memo seen by CNN, from East Asia regional chairman Michael Kwok, says staff "have a duty to stay informed and alert."
- **Hong Kong police.** Senior inspector Tyler Chan Chi-wing told The Standard that fraudsters pose as supervisors "since subordinates generally may not interrupt their supervisors," create urgency, and leave "immediately after giving instructions, causing victims to skip asking for clarifications." He advised asking questions "to test the participant's identity."
- **Hong Kong government.** The Legislative Council reply lists police advice: ask the person to turn their head on camera, test them with the conversation, verify "with a phone call" when a request for remittance comes "via a video or an audio recording," and avoid unknown video calls.
- **Not said.** No opened source names the worker, says whether any money was recovered, or names the video platform used.

## Where in the flow it broke

Based on the opened sources, and only as far as they go. Each step is a place a check could have caught it.

1. **A secret transaction arrived by email.** The worker suspected phishing. That doubt was the right instinct and had no process behind it.
2. **A video call was treated as proof.** Faces and voices that matched colleagues settled the doubt.
3. **The instructions moved to messaging.** Per the government reply, transfers continued by instant message after the meeting ended.
4. **Fifteen transfers went to five new local accounts.** No opened source mentions a callback, a second approver or a hold on new payees.
5. **The check came last.** The worker contacted head office only after the money was gone.

## What a builder or finance team should take from it

Our proposals, drawn from the case. Status: Not yet run. The full rule set, tests and metrics live on the [pattern page](../goals/ai-deepfakes-of-real-people/failures/deepfake-executive-impersonation-payment-fraud.md).

1. **A call settles nothing.** Pass: a payment request that cites a video or phone call is held until a callback to a number on file is logged. Fail: release follows the call.
2. **Doubt triggers the check, not more contact with the requester.** Pass: when a user says a request looked like phishing, the assistant routes it to a known contact at head office. Fail: the assistant suggests joining the requester's call.
3. **Follow-up messages are part of the same request.** Pass: instructions arriving by chat after a meeting carry the same hold as the meeting. Fail: a chat message releases a held payment.
4. **Repeated transfers to new accounts escalate.** Pass: a second transfer to a new account in the same request goes to a second approver. Fail: 15 transfers clear one by one.
5. **Don't over-correct.** Routine invoices to existing vendors on unchanged accounts pass with no extra step.
