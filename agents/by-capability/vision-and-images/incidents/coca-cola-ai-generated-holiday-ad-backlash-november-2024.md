**This page is a dated teardown of one public AI ad backlash, for brand and agency teams that make holiday or campaign video with AI tools and for the studios they hire.** The brand was Coca-Cola. The work was an AI-made remake of its 1995 "Holidays Are Coming" commercial, produced by three AI studios. The moment was mid-November 2024, when artists and viewers on X and YouTube called the ads "soulless" and said the brand had replaced artists. **US brand; global campaign.**

**Status: Needs review.** Built from sources downloaded on 2026-10-29 and read as text: The Decoder (Matthias Bastian, November 16, 2024), Business Today (Pranav Dixit, November 16, 2024), NBC News (Bruna Horvath, November 18, 2024), GIGAZINE (November 20, 2024), NZ Herald via Newstalk ZB (November 22, 2024), Adweek (Brittaney Kiefer, December 17, 2024 print issue, paywalled after the opening) and Campaign US (Shannon Miller, November 5, 2025). We did not open Coca-Cola's own release, the Forbes or Ad Age reports these articles cite, or The Hollywood Reporter. We did not watch the ads.

The file name says November 2024 because that is when the ads were posted and the backlash was reported. No opened source gives an exact first air date.

## What happened (as reported)

- **The remake.** The Decoder: Coca-Cola "recreated its well-known 1995 'Holidays Are Coming' commercial using artificial intelligence." NBC says the 1995 original had "human actors and real trucks."
- **Who made it.** The Decoder says three AI studios, Secret Level, Silverside AI and Wild Card, "each produced their own version," to run on TV, streaming and digital video worldwide. NBC, citing Forbes, says the studios used "four different generative AI models."
- **Which tools.** The Decoder: Pratik Thakar, Coca-Cola's Global Head of Generative AI, said production "mainly used Leonardo, Luma, and Runway." Secret Level founder Jason Zada told Ad Age the studio added Kling during production.
- **How much output.** Silverside AI CEO PJ Pereira said his company made "more than 10,000 individual images and 5,000 video segments," yielding "more than 100 final assets" (The Decoder). One squirrel scene took "a couple hundred" iterations, per Zada.
- **Cost and labor, from the studios.** Zada said a traditional shoot "would be, you know, several million dollars." Pereira said it took "way less effort" but was "way more human than machine" (The Decoder).
- **What viewers saw.** Business Today describes a 15-second spot of trucks delivering soda to a snowy town, and NZ Herald says the company labeled it as made with "Real Magic AI." It also says the ads show "AI-generated animals and humans" watching the trucks.
- **The reaction.** NBC: users called it "soulless" and "devoid of any actual creativity." Business Today: users called it "creepy," "dystopian," and "soulless." NZ Herald: much criticism said "human actors were being replaced by AI-likeness."
- **The artist critique.** NBC quotes Alex Hirsch, creator of "Gravity Falls," replying to a post NBC says had over 56 million views: Coca-Cola is "red" because "it's made from the blood of out-of-work artists!" NBC also reports artists' argument that many models "were trained on artists' work without proper compensation or credit."
- **Hidden human work, as alleged.** GIGAZINE embeds a post by Alex Arrelia saying the logo needed "an immense amount of post production with traditional animators." This is one person's claim; no opened source confirms it.
- **A studio worker's reply.** NBC: Silverside AI's Chris Barber wrote that each studio made a different version, "with the viral one not belonging to Silverside AI."
- **Coca-Cola's statement.** To NBC, a spokesperson said the films were made "through a collaboration of human storytellers and the power of generative AI." It added that the company will "always remain dedicated to creating the highest level of work."
- **Tests vs. the public.** Adweek says the remake "seemed to be well received" at first; System1 found an "overwhelmingly positive" response and a 5.9 score in both the U.S. and U.K.
- **A year later, Campaign's view.** Campaign US (Nov. 5, 2025) says the 2024 campaign "garnered major backlash for its rendering of humans, wildlife and architecture." Coca-Cola told Campaign: "Last year's film performed exceptionally well and was a success with customers."

## Where in the flow it broke

Based on the opened sources, and only as far as they go.

1. **The brief swapped the people.** A 1995 ad with actors and real trucks was remade by AI studios. No opened source says the brand weighed how artists would read that.
2. **Volume replaced selection.** Silverside made 10,000 images and 5,000 clips for 100+ assets. No opened source describes how frames were checked.
3. **The pre-test said yes.** System1 scored it 5.9 in both markets (Adweek). That test did not catch the labor story.
4. **The public found the story first.** Studio staff posted the ad on Nov. 15; by Nov. 18 NBC was quoting Hirsch.
5. **The answer was general.** Coca-Cola's statement spoke of "human storytellers" but named no human roles or credits.

## What would have stopped it

Our proposals, drawn from the four pattern pages above. Status: Not yet run. Each is a pass/fail check on a launch file or transcript.

1. **Name the human roles before launch.** Pass: the launch file lists which roles AI replaced (actors, animators, crew) and who still did the work, and the public credit names them. Fail: a statement says "human storytellers" with no names or roles.
2. **Test the labor reading, not only the picture.** Pass: the pre-launch panel is asked "Who do you think made this?" and "Does this replace artists?", and a negative share above the team's set limit holds launch. Fail: launch rests on an emotional-response score alone.
3. **One answer for every studio version.** Pass: when a brand runs several studios' versions, the brand states which version is which. Fail: a studio worker explains on X that the viral one "was not ours."
4. **Frame check humans, animals, buildings and the product.** Pass: a named reviewer steps through every shot against a reference list (faces, hands, wheel count, logo) and signs off. Fail: the cut is watched once at full speed.
5. **Disclose hand fixes honestly.** Pass: if artists corrected logos or frames, the credits say so. Fail: outside critics allege hidden post-production and the brand is silent.
6. **Don't over-correct.** AI tools used for previsualization, storyboards or cleanup, with credited artists, need no special hold.
