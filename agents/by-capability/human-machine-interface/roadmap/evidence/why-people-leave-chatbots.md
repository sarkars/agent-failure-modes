# Why People Leave Chatbots: An Evidence Review

> **Status:** v0.2, ready for review. Written 2026-10-19; every source below was opened in this revision (2026-10-19) and each row says whether it was read in full text or at abstract level only. Nothing here is our own data: no study from our [analytics plan](../chat-analytics-plan.md) has been run. Scope is text chat; voice items are labeled voice, and web or other surfaces are planned.

## The question
When people abandon a chatbot conversation, what do opened studies and regulator reports say causes it? And what do they say about whether persuasion and conversion design can change the outcome? This review is a map of the evidence we hold, with its limits.

## How to read the check labels
- **Full text:** the paper or report itself was opened and the quoted numbers were found in it.
- **Abstract only:** the publisher-derived or repository abstract was opened; the paper body was not (the journal and SSRN pages refused access). Numbers from abstract-only sources are quoted as the authors state them and carry no detail beyond the abstract.
- **Page or article:** a web page or news article was opened and read; it is guidance or reporting, not a study.

## What is not in the evidence
- **No measured drop-off rate across chatbots.** We found no open dataset of how many conversations end in abandonment, or why.
- **No A/B test of the fixes in our [patterns](../../README.md).** The fixes are reasoned from the failures and the research below; the tests in `tests/` are all "Not yet run".
- **Few studies of text chat in the wild.** Most evidence is one bank, one platform, a survey, or a regulator's reading of complaints.
- **No field replication found of the Luo disclosure result.** A search for later field experiments that re-test the purchase drop found none (see "How this was checked").

## Part 1: Causes of abandonment, by what the evidence supports
Patterns supported by Part 1: [Talk to a human ignored or looped](../../goals/human-handoff/failures/talk-to-a-human-ignored-or-looped.md), [Dead-end "I don't understand" replies](../../goals/conversation-leadership/failures/dead-end-i-don-t-understand-replies.md), [Silent long-running work](../../goals/response-time/failures/silent-long-running-work.md), [Silent failure and timeout](../../goals/response-time/failures/silent-failure-and-timeout.md), [Re-asks for known information](../../goals/embedded-copilots/failures/re-asks-for-known-information.md).

| Cause | What the opened sources say | Source (how checked) | Limits | Pattern |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **No route to a person** | The CFPB described customers stuck in "continuous loops of repetitive, unhelpful jargon or legalese without an offramp to a human customer service representative" and called them "doom loops". | [CFPB, *Chatbots in consumer finance*, June 2023, section 3.1.3](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_chatbot-issue-spotlight_2023-06.pdf) (full text; opened through an Internet Archive copy because the CFPB host refused direct download) | US consumer finance; regulator's reading of complaints with example quotes, not a measured rate | [Talk to a human ignored or looped](../../goals/human-handoff/failures/talk-to-a-human-ignored-or-looped.md) |
| **The bot does not make progress** | In a three-month log of 1,685 users (17,136 exchanges) of a banking chatbot, researchers found 12 types of non-progress and signs that users were about to abandon: three consecutive non-progress turns, consecutive reformulation or subject switching, and reformulating as the final strategy. Abandonment meant not using the bot for more than 10 days. In their data 90% of abandonments followed no more than three consecutive non-progress turns, and 45% followed just one. | [Li et al., CHI 2020](https://people.cs.nycu.edu.tw/~armuro/pubs/li-et-al-2020-chi.pdf) (full text) | One Taiwanese bank, one task-oriented chatbot; observational, so it shows association and not that a fix reduces abandonment | [Dead-end "I don't understand" replies](../../goals/conversation-leadership/failures/dead-end-i-don-t-understand-replies.md) |
| **Abandonment can be detected from those signals** | Models on an annotated log of 1,837 users detected abandonment in the moment (AUROC 90% for a BERT model, 95.7% for an attention-based model on 102 handcrafted features). For predicting abandonment earlier, the paper reports the BERT model did better. | [Yang et al., CUI 2021](https://people.cs.nycu.edu.tw/~armuro/pubs/yang-et-al-2021-cui-ea.pdf) (full text) | One bank's log, same data family as Li et al.; detection, not a demonstration that any intervention works | Same |
| **Repair offered badly** | In a scenario study with 203 Mechanical Turk workers comparing eight repair strategies, offering options and explanations was generally favored. | [Ashktorab et al., CHI 2019](https://research.ibm.com/publications/resilient-chatbots-repair-strategy-preferences-for-conversational-breakdowns) (abstract only; IBM Research page) | Hypothetical scenarios, not live use; stated preference | Same |
| **Silence, stalls, and failures with no message** | Response-time guidance says percent-done style feedback is needed for operations taking more than about 10 seconds, the limit for keeping attention on the dialogue. Vendor guidance says streamed LLM answers can be cut off by timeouts or network failures and shows checking for a missing finish signal. | [Nielsen, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/) and [Atlassian Forge docs](https://developer.atlassian.com/platform/forge/runtime-reference/forge-llms-api-errors/) (both page or article, read in full) | General interface guidance and one vendor's engineering advice; no measured effect on abandonment | [Silent long-running work](../../goals/response-time/failures/silent-long-running-work.md), [Silent failure and timeout](../../goals/response-time/failures/silent-failure-and-timeout.md) |
| **Being asked for what the bot should already know** | A Salesforce survey (Australia and New Zealand, 535 customers) found 66% often repeat information to different representatives. | [B&T article on the report](https://www.bandt.com.au/customers-expect-connected-experience-still-dealing-silos/) (page or article; the report itself was not opened) | Vendor survey reported secondhand; about people and departments, not bots | [Re-asks for known information](../../goals/embedded-copilots/failures/re-asks-for-known-information.md) |
| **Fear of losing the route to a person** | A Gartner survey of 5,728 customers (December 2023) found 64% would prefer companies not use AI in customer service and 53% would consider switching if a company would use it. Gartner reports the top concern is that AI will make it harder to reach a person. | Gartner press release, 9 July 2024 (page or article; read in the full republished text at [Insurance-Canada.ca](https://insurance-canada.ca/2024/07/15/gartner-survey-ai-for-customer-service/) because gartner.com showed a bot check; the Gartner copy itself was not opened) | Vendor survey of stated attitudes, not behavior; republished copy, not the original page | [Talk to a human ignored or looped](../../goals/human-handoff/failures/talk-to-a-human-ignored-or-looped.md) |

**What the table says:** the strongest evidence is for the dead end and the missing route to a person (full-text sources plus the regulator). For the rest, the evidence is guidance, stated preference, or vendor surveys, so the patterns present them as likely causes to test, not proven drivers of drop-off.

## Part 2: Does disclosing a bot, or making it persuasive, change outcomes?
Patterns supported by Part 2: [Undisclosed bot identity](../../goals/trust-and-rapport/failures/undisclosed-bot-identity.md) (disclosure and trust) and [Help that becomes a sales pitch](../../goals/embedded-copilots/failures/help-that-becomes-a-sales-pitch.md) (persuasion in a support setting). The studies do not test either pattern's fix.

Three studies bear on the "boring bot" and conversion question.

| Study | What it reports | Source (how checked) | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Luo, Tong, Fang and Qu (2019)** | A field experiment on more than 6,200 customers randomized to highly structured outbound sales calls from chatbots or human workers. Undisclosed chatbots were as effective as proficient workers and four times more effective than inexperienced workers; disclosing the chatbot's identity before the conversation reduced purchase rates by more than 79.7%. Customers who knew it was a machine were curt and perceived it as less knowledgeable and less empathetic. The authors report the negative effect can be mitigated by a late disclosure timing and by customers' prior AI experience. | *Marketing Science* 38(6), 937-947, DOI 10.1287/mksc.2019.1192, [abstract on IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v38y2019i6p937-947.html) (abstract only; the INFORMS PDF refused access and Unpaywall lists no open copy) | Outbound sales calls, not open chat, and not shown to be text. A secondary source describes a financial-services company in Asia; the abstract does not name the setting. Disclosure rules and customer attitudes may have changed since 2019. We cannot say "do not disclose": disclosure may be required by law in some places, which is a question for counsel. |
| **Xu, Dai and Yan (*Management Science*, 2026)** | In a field experiment with a large truck-sharing platform, 11,000 truck drivers were randomly assigned to outbound calls from a voice chatbot dispatcher. Disclosing the chatbot's identity at the start led to around 11% reduction in response probability. Adding interjections and filler words raised response probability, conversation length, and order-acceptance intention by over 5.6%, 24.9%, and 10.1%. With disclosure and these features together, outcomes still improved. The authors propose enhanced trust as one plausible explanation. | DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2022.03833 (Crossref record shows issued January 2026). Abstract text from the OpenAlex record of the DOI, matching the [SUFE/GLOBE seminar listing](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/386643); press summary in the [UNC Kenan-Flagler article](https://www.kenan-flagler.unc.edu/news/making-voice-chatbots-more-effective-by-design/) (abstract only; the INFORMS page, SSRN and ResearchGate refused access, Unpaywall points only to the SSRN preprint) | **Voice**, not text chat. One platform (a Chinese freight platform per the press summary) and one population of drivers. Figures come from the abstract; the paper body was not read, so sample details and statistical tests are unchecked. One secondary listing gives a different upper range for one effect; we quote the abstract's figures only. |
| **Salvi, Horta Ribeiro, Gallotti and West (*Nature Human Behaviour*, 2025)** | A preregistered study (N = 900) of short multiround debates, randomized into 12 conditions across human or GPT-4 opponent, with or without access to the participant's sociodemographic data, and three topic opinion strengths. In debate pairs where AI and humans were not equally persuasive, GPT-4 with personalization was more persuasive 64.4% of the time (81.2% relative increase in odds of higher post-debate agreement; 95% CI +26.0% to +160.7%; P < 0.01). | [Nature Human Behaviour article, vol. 9, 1645-1653](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02194-6) (full text; the PDF was opened and the figures found in it). The earlier [arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14380) (full text) reports 81.7% higher odds with N = 820; use the journal figures. | Participants were US adults recruited through Prolific, debating opinion topics in a controlled setting (median completion time about 16 minutes); not selling or support. The authors frame it as a governance and misuse concern. It shows personalization can raise persuasion in debate, not that it raises conversion in a business chat. |

**What this means for design, with limits.**
- The two field experiments both show that telling people they are talking to a machine lowers purchase or response in those settings. Neither tells you to hide it. Honest disclosure plus better conversation design is the position the Xu result supports, and it is voice evidence, abstract level.
- Style choices (filler words in voice, empathy, timing of disclosure) moved outcomes in those studies. That supports testing wording and flow as variables, which is the job of the [analytics plan's studies](../chat-analytics-plan.md). It does not give us a recipe for text chat.
- Persuasion research is an argument for guardrails as much as for conversion. Our persuasion layer, if built, is gated by disclosure and consent rules ([distribution plan](../distribution-plan.md)).

## Part 3: What to publish and what to test next
Patterns this section points to: the same dead-end and human-handoff patterns as Part 1.
1. Publish this page with its limits, as the first public statement of what is and is not known.
2. Use Part 1 to choose which failures the [studies S0-S6](../chat-analytics-plan.md) test first: dead ends and the missing route to a person have the strongest prior evidence, so a simulated study can ask whether our proposed detection signals find them.
3. Do not claim any conversion result. We have none.
