**This page is a dated teardown of one US lawsuit over AI answers in a search engine that, the plaintiff alleges, told people a small business had been sued by the state attorney general when it had not, for teams whose chatbot or answer engine summarizes what the web says about a named company or person.** Every fact below comes from a source we opened (listed at the end). The channel is text: a written AI answer at the top of a search results page.

**Read this first.** This page summarizes public reporting and court filings. Everything the complaint says is an allegation. Google denied the allegations in its answer, as the Minnesota Star Tribune reports. No court has ruled on whether the answers were false, defamatory, or caused any loss; the only ruling we found is about which court hears the case. This is design analysis, not legal advice. Quotes of Google's answers come from the text of the complaint, not from screenshots we checked. **Status: Needs review. Last updated: 2026-10-26.**

## What happened (as reported)

From the complaint (Ramsey County, Minnesota; filed on the federal docket on June 12, 2025, as Doc. 11), unless another source is named:

- **The company.** LTL LED, LLC, doing business as Wolf River Electric, sells, designs, installs and repairs solar systems. The complaint says: "Wolf River is not a financial lending company."
- **September 2, 2024: discovery.** The complaint says that "on or around" this date, employees found Google stating that Wolf River was "facing a lawsuit from the Minnesota Attorney General due to violating several laws."
- **What the first answer said.** For the search "wolf river electric lawsuit," the complaint quotes Google as saying that, "According to recent news reports," Wolf River "is currently facing a lawsuit from the Minnesota Attorney General" over "deceptive sales practices." It then listed "Key points about the lawsuit," including "Deceptive marketing," "High-pressure tactics," "Hidden fees," and "Installation issues."
- **The cited sources.** The complaint says the answer cited four sources: a Minnesota Star Tribune article on hidden solar fees, an Angie's List review, a Minnesota Attorney General publication, and a KROC News article. It says "None of the four sources Google cited support this claim." It says the Attorney General publication and the KROC article never mention Wolf River. The Star Tribune (Emmy Martin, June 13, 2025) gives one more detail: its own cited story "mentioned Wolf River Electric at the end, but did not say the company was included in the lawsuit."
- **Other searches, other versions.** The complaint quotes four more answers, each with different details:
  - For "Wolf River Electric lawsuit Minnesota Settlement," the office "has obtained consent judgments against solar installers and lenders, returning over $300,000 to consumers," and it named Wolf River among "solar lending companies."
  - For "Wolf River Electric lawsuit update today," the Attorney General sued in April 2022 and "the case is still ongoing."
  - For "Wolf River Electric sued by attorney general," the answer said that "In March 2024, Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison filed a lawsuit against several solar lending companies, including Wolf River Electric."
  - For "Wolf River Electric company legit lawsuit," the answer said the company's practices "are currently being investigated by legal authorities."
- **The real suit.** The complaint quotes the Attorney General's release on the March 2024 suit: it named "GoodLeap, Sunlight Financial, Solar Mosaic, and Dividend Solar Finance." The Star Tribune says Wolf River "was not a defendant in that suit."
- **Autocomplete.** The complaint says that typing "Wolf River Electric" brought up suggestions such as "Wolf River Electric lawsuit," "Wolf River Electric lawsuit update today," and "Wolf River Electric lawsuit Minnesota settlement." Picking them led to the answers above.
- **What the answers told readers to do.** One answer ended: "consider contacting the Minnesota Attorney General's office to file a complaint." The complaint says several customers did file complaints, and that Wolf River believes Google sent them there.
- **The disclaimer.** The complaint says the answer carried the note "Generative AI is experimental. For legal advice, consult a professional." It says people saw it only after clicking "Show more."
- **What the company says it lost.** The complaint lists, all in March 2025:
  - March 3: a customer canceled a $39,680.00 contract and "referred to lawsuits that appear when he 'Googled' Wolf River."
  - March 4: a prospect turned down a $26,400.00 proposal.
  - March 5: a customer canceled a $150,000.00 contract even though Wolf River's CEO told him the claims were false.
  - March 11: a nonprofit said it was "pulling the plug" because of "several lawsuits in the last year" with the "Attorney General's Office." It ended a $147,400.00 solar project and a $26,644.12 lighting project.

  The complaint also cites Reddit posts repeating the claim.
- **The counts.** Defamation, defamation per se, defamation by implication, a Minnesota Deceptive Trade Practices Act claim, and a request for a declaration that the posts must be removed. Each defamation count asks for "in excess of $50,000."
- **How the suit began.** The summons is dated March 11, 2025. Google's notice of removal says Wolf River "commenced this action by sending via certified mail" the papers that day. Google's brief says Wolf River did not file the complaint in court and relied on Minnesota's "pocket service" procedure. The remand order says the papers were served on March 14, 2025, with a letter claiming "$24.7 million in damages in 2024 due to Google's defamation."
- **Google's answer and the amended complaint.** Google's notice says it served an answer on April 22, 2025. On May 23, 2025, an amended complaint added four people as plaintiffs: Justin Nielsen, Vladimir Marchenko, Luka Bozek and Jonathan Latcham. On June 3, 2025, plaintiffs' initial disclosures put Wolf River's damages at $110,000,000 to $210,000,000.
- **June 9, 2025: removal.** Google removed the case to the US District Court for the District of Minnesota (No. 25-cv-2394), citing diversity of citizenship. The Star Tribune reports that Wolf River's general counsel, Nick Kasprowicz, said the matter "was never about publicity," and that Google's move to federal court made the suit public.
- **Google's response.** The Star Tribune says Google's response denied the allegations and said the AI Overview describes "documents, publications, and internet postings, the contents of which speak for themselves." The Star Tribune says Google did not respond to its request for comment. It also says: "The AI Overview has since been removed." PPC Land (Luís Rijo, November 14, 2025) quotes a Google statement that "with any new technology, mistakes can happen" and that Google "acted quickly to fix it." PPC Land does not say where or when Google said this.
- **Later sighting.** PPC Land says that on November 11, 2025, a Google search for "wolf river electric complaint" still said the company "is also facing a lawsuit from the Minnesota attorney general related to its sales practices." We did not repeat that search.
- **January 9, 2026: remand.** Judge Jeffrey M. Bryan granted the motion to remand. He ruled that the March letter put Google on notice that more than $75,000 was at stake. He said Google removed "long after the thirty-day removal period had expired," and ordered: "This case is REMANDED to Ramsey County District Court." He denied Google's motion to amend its notice. The CourtListener docket shows the clerk sent the order to Ramsey County on January 12, 2026, the county acknowledged it on January 20, and the federal case is marked terminated on February 26, 2026.
- **After remand.** We found no opened source on what has happened in the state court since January 2026.

## What the user saw

The user is a homeowner or a nonprofit choosing a solar installer. They type the company's name, and Google suggests "lawsuit" searches. They pick one. At the top of the page a fluent paragraph says, "According to recent news reports," the state attorney general is suing the company. Bold headings follow, and links to a newspaper, a review site and the attorney general's own website. The answer even tells them where to file a complaint. The links look like support. The complaint says none of them backs the central claim. The note that the answer was "experimental" was hidden behind "Show more," the complaint says. Wolf River was not the user. It found out from its own staff and from customers who canceled.

## Where in the flow it broke

Based on the complaint, unless noted:

1. **Query suggestion.** Autocomplete offered "lawsuit," "settlement" and "sued by attorney general" searches for a company that, per the complaint, was never sued by the state.
2. **Premise taken as fact.** A search phrased "sued by attorney general" got an answer that confirmed the suit, not one that checked it.
3. **Entity mix-up.** The answers attached a real suit against other companies, lenders, to an installer named nearby in one article (the Star Tribune's account of its own story).
4. **Citation check.** The answer cited four sources for a claim the complaint says none of them makes.
5. **Different stories per wording.** Five searches gave different years (2022, 2024), different roles (installer, lender) and different outcomes (ongoing, consent judgments).
6. **Call to action.** The answer told readers to file complaints against the company.
7. **Fix coverage.** The Star Tribune says the overview was removed. PPC Land says a similar claim still showed up for a different wording in November 2025.

## How this differs from Starbuck, Walters and noyb

- [Starbuck v. Meta](meta-ai-starbuck-defamation-lawsuit-false-january-6-claims-april-2025.md): a public figure, a chatbot, and repeats after notice. Here the plaintiff is a business that Volokh says "appears not to be a public figure." The answers sat in search results, and the complaint names specific canceled contracts. Starbuck settled. This case is back in state court with no ruling on the merits in what we opened.
- [Walters v. OpenAI](chatgpt-false-embezzlement-claim-walters-v-openai-defamation-ruling-may-2025.md): one reader who did not believe the claim, and a ruling for OpenAI. Here the complaint names customers who, it alleges, believed the answers and canceled.
- [noyb's complaint over ChatGPT](chatgpt-false-claims-about-a-person-noyb-complaint-march-2025.md): a private person and a GDPR accuracy complaint to a regulator. Both cases say a visible answer was fixed while similar claims may remain.
- [Google AI Overviews' odd answers (May 2024)](google-ai-overviews-odd-and-unsafe-answers-may-2024.md): the same feature. There the bad answers were mostly odd advice on rare queries. Here the complaint says the answers were about one named business, on searches its customers ran.

## Pattern match

Taxonomy: [failure taxonomy v1](../roadmap/specs/failure-taxonomy-v1.md).

- **[Confident wrong answer, no way to check](../goals/errors-and-recovery/failures/confident-wrong-answer-with-no-way-to-check.md)** (`confident-wrong-answer-with-no-way-to-check`). Strong fit. A false claim of a government lawsuit was stated as fact, opened with "According to recent news reports," with no sign of doubt. The answer did cite sources. But the complaint says they did not support the claim, so a reader who checked would have had to read all four to find that out.
- **[False premises accepted](../goals/dialogue-management/failures/false-premises-accepted.md)** (`false-premises-accepted`). Partial fit. The search "Wolf River Electric sued by attorney general" assumes a suit, and the answer confirmed it instead of checking. Partial, because that page covers a user's own wrong belief in a chat, and here the wording was offered by autocomplete.
- **[Starter suggestions that fail](../goals/onboarding-and-discoverability/failures/starter-suggestions-that-fail.md)** (`starter-suggestions-that-fail`). Partial fit. The product suggested searches ("lawsuit update today," "lawsuit Minnesota settlement"), and each one led, per the complaint, to a false answer. That page says a suggestion is a promise the system can answer the question. Partial, because these were search autocompletes, not chat starter chips.
- **[Corrections not applied](../goals/dialogue-management/failures/corrections-not-applied.md)** (`corrections-not-applied`). Weak fit. The Star Tribune says the overview was removed, and PPC Land says a similar claim appeared for another search in November 2025. Weak, because that page covers a correction inside one chat. This would be a fix that did not cover reworded searches, and it rests on one report we did not repeat.
- **Considered and rejected.** [One-sided or cherry-picked sources](../goals/research-and-data-agents/failures/one-sided-or-cherry-picked-sources.md) (`one-sided-or-cherry-picked-sources`): the problem was sources that did not say what the answer claimed, not a lack of balance. [Excess disclaimers and warnings](../goals/refusals-and-boundaries/failures/excess-disclaimers-and-warnings.md) (`excess-disclaimers-and-warnings`): the complaint describes the opposite, one disclaimer, hidden. [No way to report bad behaviour](../goals/measurement-and-feedback/failures/no-way-to-report-bad-behaviour.md) (`no-way-to-report-bad-behaviour`): no opened source says whether Wolf River tried Google's feedback tools before suing.

## Possible gaps for a person to judge

These are proposals, not decisions.

1. As the Starbuck, Walters and noyb pages note, no pattern covers false accusations about a named third party. This case adds a business, not a person.
2. No pattern covers a citation that does not support the sentence it sits under. The citation looks like proof the claim was checked.
3. No pattern covers entity merging: a fact about one company (a suit against lenders) attached to another company named in the same source.
4. No pattern covers an answer that tells readers to take action against the subject, here filing complaints with a regulator.
5. No pattern covers a disclaimer hidden behind "Show more" on the answer it qualifies.

## What to measure

Our proposals, not Google's. Status: Not yet run. Thresholds are for the team to set.

| Signal | How to compute | Counter-signal |
|---|---|---|
| Unsupported legal claims | Answers that say a named company or person was sued, charged, fined or settled, where no cited page contains both the name and the legal action, divided by all such answers | Real suits reported only in sources the system cannot read, which a strict check would hide |
| Citation support rate | For a sample of answers about named entities, share of claim sentences whose cited page states the claim, checked by a person | Claims that need two sources together, which a one-page check marks as failing |
| Cross-wording consistency | For one entity, run a fixed set of wordings ("lawsuit," "sued by," "settlement," "complaint," "legit") and count answers that disagree on who sued, when, or the outcome | Real changes in a case over time, which should change answers |
| Premise confirmation | Share of "was X sued by Y" searches answered yes where no cited source names X as a party | Searches where the answer says "no record found," which should count as passing |
| Suggested-query harm | Share of autocomplete suggestions with "lawsuit," "scam" or "sued" for a named business whose AI answer fails the support check | Suggestions that reflect real, sourced suits |
| Post-fix recurrence | After a reported claim is removed, share of the fixed wording set that still produces it, run daily for 30 days | New true reporting that appears after the fix |

## What a team can change

Our proposals, each a pass/fail check on a logged answer. Status: Not yet run.

1. **No lawsuit claim without a party match.** Pass: an answer says a named company was sued only if a cited page names that company as a party to that suit. Fail: the company appears in the source only nearby, as in the Star Tribune's account of its own story.
2. **Check the premise, not just the topic.** Pass: "Was Wolf River sued by the attorney general?" with no supporting source gets "I found no record of that suit" and the real defendants if known. Fail: the answer confirms the suit.
3. **One story across wordings.** Pass: the five searches quoted in the complaint give answers that agree on parties, year and outcome, or decline. Fail: two answers give different years or roles.
4. **No calls to action against a subject.** Pass: an answer about a named business does not tell the reader to file a complaint against it unless a cited official source does. Fail: it does.
5. **Show the limit where the answer is read.** Pass: a note that the answer may be wrong sits next to the answer, not behind "Show more," on any answer that makes a legal claim about a named entity.
6. **Fix the claim, then test the wordings.** Pass: after a reported false claim is removed, the fixed wording set, including suggested searches, returns no version of it.
