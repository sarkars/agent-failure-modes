**This page is a dated teardown of two federal court orders in 2025 that judges later tied to staff use of general AI chat tools, for teams that build, buy or set rules for a chat assistant whose drafts can become an official government or legal record.** The products were two general chat assistants, Perplexity and OpenAI's ChatGPT, used inside judges' chambers. The users were a law clerk and a law school intern. The moment that matters is not the chat itself but what came after it: AI-assisted text went into a draft order, and the draft was entered on the public court docket before the review steps the judges say normally catch errors. The date above is when Grassley's office released the judges' letters; the orders themselves are dated June 30 and July 20, 2025.

**Status: Needs review.** Six sources were downloaded on 2026-10-28 as raw HTML and read as plain text: FedScoop (Madison Alder, October 23, 2025), a Reuters report republished by Business Insurance (October 23, 2025, no byline shown), the ABA Journal (Debra Cassens Weiss, October 24, 2025, modified October 27, 2025), Fox News (Landon Mion, October 24, 2025), WLOX/WLBT (Anthony Warren, October 8, 2025) and Broadband Breakfast (October 27, 2025, no byline seen in the text). Each quote below was checked word for word against the downloaded text with a search, with curly and straight quote marks treated as the same. The Senate Judiciary Committee's own press release and the judges' letters could not be downloaded (the request was blocked, and an archive copy was also blocked); we quote the letters only as the outlets above quote them.

> **Read this first.** This is a summary of public reporting, not a finding of fact, and not legal advice. It does not say what any court rule, ethics rule or law requires, and it does not judge the merits of either case. We did not read either withdrawn order, the corrected orders, the chats with Perplexity or ChatGPT, or the judges' letters themselves. The clerk and the intern are not named in any opened source, and we do not name them. We name only people who spoke on record or acted in an official role: Judge Henry T. Wingate, Judge Julien Xavier Neals and Senator Chuck Grassley.

**Channel note.** This is a text incident: court staff typed into general web chat assistants and copied what they got into a draft. This phase of the knowledge base is chat-first, and the signals and checks below are written for chat logs and the documents drafted from them. Voice and web signals, such as a dictation tool that feeds a draft, or a public docket page that shows AI-drafted text, are PLANNED only: never detected or measured here.

## What happened (as reported)

- **June 30, 2025: the New Jersey order.** Per the ABA Journal, Neals's "June 30 opinion denying a motion to dismiss misstated case outcomes and used fake quotes attributed to opinions and the defendants." Reuters (via Business Insurance) and Fox News say it was a draft decision in a securities lawsuit.
- **July 20, 2025: the Mississippi order.** Per WLOX, citing Grassley's release, Wingate's order granted a temporary restraining order to pause a law that prohibits DEI programs in public schools. Per the ABA Journal, it "referred to nonexistent allegations, parties and declarations."
- **Who found the errors.** Per the ABA Journal, both judges "withdrew the opinions after litigants pointed out the errors." Per WLOX, the Mississippi Attorney General's Office filed a motion saying the order misquoted state law, made inaccurate statements not supported in the record, and referenced individuals who were not party to the case.
- **The first explanation in Mississippi.** Per Reuters, Wingate removed and replaced the original order and at first declined to explain, saying it contained "clerical errors." Grassley's letter, as WLOX quotes it, says Wingate "declined to explain the errors or keep the original order publicly available on the docket."
- **October 2025: the Senate letters.** WLOX reported on October 8, 2025 that Grassley asked both judges to "explain whether they, their law clerks, or any court staff used generative AI" in preparing their decisions, and to "re-docket their original orders to preserve a transparent history of the courts' actions." He asked Wingate to answer by October 13, including whether privileged information was entered into an AI program.
- **What Wingate said.** Per Reuters, a clerk used Perplexity "as a foundational drafting assistant to synthesize publicly available information on the docket." FedScoop adds the word "strictly" and says the clerk did not enter sensitive or non-public information. Wingate called posting the draft "a lapse in human oversight," per Reuters.
- **The skipped review, per Wingate.** "The standard practice in my chambers is for every draft opinion to undergo several levels of review before becoming final and being docketed, including the use of cite checking tools," he wrote, per FedScoop. The July 20 order "was an early draft that had not gone through the standard review process."
- **What Neals said.** Per the ABA Journal, a law school intern used ChatGPT to perform legal research: "In doing so, the intern acted without authorization, without disclosure, and contrary to not only chambers policy but also the relevant law school policy." Per Reuters, the draft "was released in error – human error – and withdrawn as soon as it was brought to the attention of my chambers."
- **A rule that existed only by word of mouth.** Neals, per Fox News: "My chamber's policy prohibits the use of GenAI in the legal research for, or drafting of, opinions or orders," and "In the past, my policy was communicated verbally to chamber's staff, including interns. That is no longer the case."

## What the organizations said

- **Judge Wingate.** "It was a draft that should have never been docketed," and "This was a mistake. I have taken steps in my chambers to ensure this mistake will not happen again" (ABA Journal). His new rule, per FedScoop: "all draft opinions, orders, and memorandum decisions undergo a mandatory, independent review by a second law clerk before submission to me. All cited cases are printed from Westlaw and attached to a final draft." Per Broadband Breakfast, he said "it would be confusing to leave a flawed opinion on the record."
- **Judge Neals.** He now has "a written unequivocal policy that applies to all law clerks and interns, pending definitive guidance from the AO through adoption of formal, universal policies and procedures for appropriate AI usage" (FedScoop). Both judges said they posted docket notices that the inaccurate opinion had been removed, per the ABA Journal.
- **Senator Grassley.** "Honesty is always the best policy" (Fox News). "We can't allow laziness, apathy or overreliance on artificial assistance to upend the Judiciary's commitment to integrity and factual accuracy" (FedScoop, Fox News).
- **Perplexity, to FedScoop.** Its tool "never claims to be 100% accurate," and "we strongly advise all AI users to check the work of their AI assistants." The spokesperson also said "today we were 1.84% inaccurate"; that is the company's own claim, and no opened source tests it.
- **OpenAI.** Per FedScoop, it did not immediately respond to a request for comment.
- **Administrative Office of the US Courts.** Per Broadband Breakfast, it said its AI Task Force issued interim guidance in July urging judges to confirm and, where appropriate, disclose any use of AI tools. We did not open that guidance.

## Where in the flow it broke

Based on the sources above, and only as far as they go.

1. **A tool outside the rules.** In New Jersey, the intern used ChatGPT against a policy that, until then, was only spoken, per Neals.
2. **Output that reads like law.** The drafts held quotes attributed to opinions and parties, and named parties and declarations, in the same voice as accurate text. No opened source says the tools flagged any of it as uncertain.
3. **No source check before the draft moved on.** Wingate says his normal process includes cite checking tools; this draft had not been through it.
4. **The draft became the record.** Both judges say an early draft was docketed as a final order.
5. **Outsiders caught it.** Litigants, including the Mississippi Attorney General's Office, pointed out the errors, per the ABA Journal and WLOX.
6. **The first explanation.** In Mississippi, the order was replaced with a note of "clerical errors," per Reuters; AI use was disclosed only after the Senate letter.

## What to measure

Proposed signals, not results. Status: Not yet run. Text signals are proposed; voice and web signals are PLANNED only and are not detected or measured. Each can be computed from chat logs, document drafts and a labeled review set; each has a counter-signal.

| Signal | How to compute from logs | Counter-signal |
|---|---|---|
| Unresolvable quote rate | Share of quoted passages in AI-assisted drafts that a search of the cited opinion or record does not find word for word | Share of drafts that drop quotes entirely to pass the check |
| Unmatched party or filing names | Share of party, declaration or allegation names in a draft that do not appear in the case record | Share of true names wrongly flagged (spelling variants, later filings) |
| Review gate skipped | Share of documents published to the official record without a logged second review | Time from final draft to publication, so the gate does not stall urgent orders |
| Unapproved tool use | Share of drafts whose edit history shows text pasted from a tool not on the approved list | Share of staff who stop logging tool use at all |
| Correction disclosure | Share of withdrawn or replaced documents whose notice says what was wrong and how it was made | Share of notices so long that readers skip them |

## What a team can change

Our proposals, not the judges' or the courts'. Each is a pass/fail check on a chat log, a draft or a publish event. Status: Not yet run. Test sizes and thresholds are for the team to set.

1. **Every quote resolves.** Pass: each quotation in a draft matches the cited source word for word, with the source attached. Fail: any quote with no match reaches a reviewer unmarked.
2. **Every name is in the record.** Pass: each party, declaration and allegation named in a draft is found in the case record. Fail: a name with no match.
3. **No publish without a logged review.** Pass: the system will not post a document to the official record until a second person's review is logged. Fail: a draft is posted with no review entry.
4. **Mark AI-assisted text in the draft.** Pass: text that came from a chat tool is marked as such in the draft until a person checks it. Fail: pasted AI text that looks the same as checked text.
5. **Written rules, approved tools, kept logs.** Pass: staff get a written AI policy, the approved tools keep chat logs the office can review, and use of other tools is blocked or logged. Fail: a rule that exists only by word of mouth.
6. **Say what went wrong.** Pass: a notice for a withdrawn document says what kind of error it held and whether an AI tool was used. Fail: "clerical errors" with no detail when AI use is known.
